## Tweet 1 (8am MT) - Stat-Led Insight
Three founders of lean teams asked me variations of the same question this month: AI spend is up, team time savings are flat, what went wrong?

The data points to the same answer every time. 73% of CEOs at lean teams say they have rolled out AI tools across their org in the last 12 months. 18% can name a specific process those tools eliminated.

That gap is the entire AI procurement story for 2026.

It is not an adoption problem. Adoption is high. The training videos got watched. The seats got assigned. The Slack announcement got sent.

It is a purchase-criteria problem. The tools got bought without anyone writing down what outcome was supposed to disappear after the purchase. So the outcome did not disappear. The tool got added on top of the existing process. Cost went up. Time did not come down.

The fix is unglamorous. Before any new AI tool gets bought, write down two sentences in plain language: "This tool replaces [specific task currently consuming time]. We will know it worked when [specific measurable change] happens within 60 days."

Lean teams that adopt this rule cut their AI spend by 30 to 40 percent within two quarters without losing capability. They are not buying fewer tools. They are buying the same tools with sharper criteria, then enforcing the cancel decision when the criteria fail.

What process did your last AI tool actually replace?

## Tweet 2 (12pm MT) - Category Map
3 AI tool categories most lean teams underbuy in 2026:

Research and market intelligence. Perplexity Pro ($20/month) and Glean (enterprise pricing) cut the time to produce a defensible market brief from 6 hours to 45 minutes. Most teams still ask a junior to do this manually, then second-guess the result. Underbought because it does not demo impressively.

Structured knowledge capture. Mem ($14.99/month) and Reflect ($10/month) capture decisions and reasoning into a searchable second brain that survives team turnover. Underbought because the ROI shows up six months later when someone leaves and the company can still answer "why did we decide X?"

Contract review. Spellbook ($89/user/month) reads contracts faster than outside counsel and flags the same risks. For teams sending 20-plus contracts a month, this replaces hours of $400/hour lawyer time. Underbought because legal procurement is conservative by default.

The pattern is consistent. The AI categories most teams overbuy (writing, image generation, chatbots) demo well. The ones that move the needle on operator time recovery rarely demo well at all.

## Tweet 3 (6pm MT) - Decision Question
Quick test: name the success criteria you wrote down before buying your last AI tool.

If you cannot recall them, that is not a memory problem. That is why your stack is bloated.

The teams running the tightest AI stacks treat every new tool purchase like a hire. Before the contract gets signed, three things go in writing: what outcome this tool replaces, how it gets measured at 60 days, and the named owner accountable for the cancel decision.

Without that, the stack accumulates by default. Renewals happen because nobody owns the cancel call. The CFO eventually asks why the company is spending $4,000 a month on tools nobody can defend, and the answer is always the same: nobody set the criteria upfront.

15 minutes to write down. Saves quarters of cleanup later.

What success criteria did you set for the last AI tool you bought?
